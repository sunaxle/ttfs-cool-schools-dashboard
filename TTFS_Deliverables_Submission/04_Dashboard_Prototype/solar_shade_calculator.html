The Tale of the Morning Monster and the Tiny Noon Hat

Imagine holding a flashlight in a dark room and pointing it at a rubber ball or your favorite teddy bear on the floor:

  • In the early morning (8:00 AM): The sun is waking up low on the horizon, shining from the side like someone holding a flashlight near the carpet. The ball's shadow stretches out super long—like a giant, stretched-out monster shadow across your whole room!
  • At lunch time (12:00 Noon): The sun climbs directly above your head in the sky, pointing straight down like a lamp on the ceiling. The shadow has nowhere to stretch, so it shrinks into a tiny, neat little circle right under the tree, just like wearing a sun hat on your head!
  • In the evening (6:00 PM): The sun dips low again on the other side of the sky, and whoosh! The shadow stretches out into a long giant again, pointing east!
🌟 The Big Secret: The shadow is smallest when the sun is straight above you at noon, and biggest in the morning and evening! But the noon shadow is the coolest and darkest place to take a nap because it blocks the strongest sunburn rays!

Sun Angles, Similar Triangles, and the Elongating Ellipse

When parallel rays of sunlight strike a round tree canopy, the shadow on the ground is formed by geometry and similar right triangles:

  • The Sun's Elevation Angle (α): This is the angle between the flat ground and the sun in the sky. At 6:00 AM, the angle is 0°. By 12:00 Noon, the angle reaches its peak (around 80° to 85° in South Texas in summer). By 8:00 PM, it drops back down to 0°.
  • Why the Shadow Stretches: The length of the shadow cast by the tree top is given by the tangent ratio:
    Shadow Length L = Tree Height H / tan(α)
    When the sun is low at 8:00 AM (α = 25°), tan(25°) ≈ 0.466. A 30-foot tree casts a shadow that is 30 / 0.466 ≈ 64.3 feet long!
    At 12:00 Noon (α = 82°), tan(82°) ≈ 7.11. The same 30-foot tree casts a shadow that is only 30 / 7.11 ≈ 4.2 feet long!
  • Circle to Ellipse: At noon, the shadow is almost a perfect circle (Area = π R²). As the sun lowers, that circle is stretched out in the direction of the sunlight, turning into an ellipse whose major axis gets longer and longer!
Shadow Area at Angle α ≈ Canopy Area / sin(α) = π R² / sin(α)

Solar Coordinates, Trigonometric Projection, and Extinction Physics

To accurately calculate the shade of a 20-year-old tree throughout the day, we model the sun's position using spherical solar coordinates:

  • Solar Altitude Angle α(t) and Azimuth ψ(t):
    sin α = sin φ sin δ + cos φ cos δ cos ω(t)
    where φ = 26.2° N (UTRGV Rio Grande Valley latitude), δ is the solar declination, and ω(t) = 15° × (t - 12) is the solar hour angle.
  • Planar Projection of an Ellipsoidal Canopy: Approximating the crown as a rotational ellipsoid with horizontal radius R and vertical semi-axis b, the boundary of the cast shadow on the ground plane is an ellipse with semi-minor axis a_s = R and elongated semi-major axis:
    b_s(α) = sqrt(R² + (b / tan α)²)
    The projected ground shadow area is:
    A_ground(α) = π a_s b_s = π R sqrt(R² + b² cot² α)
  • Beer-Lambert Canopy Light Extinction: The shade darkness and cooling power depend on the Leaf Area Index (LAI) and canopy optical depth:
    I_understory = I_direct × exp(-k × LAI / sin α)
    where k ≈ 0.65 for Live Oak and k ≈ 0.40 for Mesquite. At noon, I_direct ≈ 1000 W/m². The Live Oak intercepts >94% of this energy, producing a ΔT ≈ 22-26°F surface drop on asphalt.

Differential Geometry, Vector Flux Integrals, and Area Rate of Change

Let the tree canopy be bounded by the smooth surface S ⊂ ℝ³. The parallel solar irradiance vector is given by the unit direction ŝ(t) = ⟨ cosαcosψ, cosαsinψ, sinα ⟩.

1. Area Transformation via Jacobian Determinant:
The projection mapping Π: ℝ³ → Ground Plane (z=0) along direction ŝ satisfies the differential relation:
dA_ground = dA_⊥ / (ŝ · k̂) = dA_⊥ / sin(α) = csc(α) dA_⊥
where dA_⊥ is the cross-sectional area orthogonal to the solar rays. For a spherical canopy of radius R, A_⊥ = π R² for all α, yielding:
A(t) = π R² csc(α(t))
2. Time Derivative and Critical Extremum:
Applying the chain rule to compute the instantaneous rate of shade footprint expansion/contraction:
dA/dt = (dA/dα) · (dα/dt) = (-π R² csc α cot α) · α'(t)
Setting dA/dt = 0 for critical points:
α'(t) = 0 ⇒ t = t_solar_noon
Since d²A/dα² = π R² csc α (csc² α + cot² α) > 0 for α ∈ (0, π/2], and α''(t_noon) < 0, the Second Derivative Test confirms that Ground Shade Area achieves a strict Global Minimum at Solar Noon:
A_min = A(t_noon) = π R² csc(α_max)
3. Total Diurnal Radiant Energy Interception Integral:
The cumulative thermal solar energy intercepted by the tree crown from sunrise to sunset is:
E_total = ∫ ∫ I_0(t) (ŝ(t) · n̂) dA dt = ∫ I_0(t) · π R² dt ≈ 28.4 kWh/day
