# Urban Development and Tree Equity in the Rio Grande Valley: A Focus on Donna and Mercedes, TX

## 1. Executive Summary
The Rio Grande Valley (RGV), particularly Hidalgo County, is experiencing rapid urbanization and increasingly severe heat events. This report analyzes the historical urban development and current "Tree Equity" of the region, focusing on the communities of Donna and Mercedes. Decades of agricultural clearing followed by dense urban development with insufficient green infrastructure investment have left these cities vulnerable to the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect. Lower-income neighborhoods disproportionately bear the brunt of these climatic challenges due to a stark lack of urban tree canopy.

## 2. Tree Equity Scores in Hidalgo County
The **Tree Equity Score (TES)**, developed by American Forests, measures how well the benefits of urban tree canopy—such as cooling, air quality improvement, and stormwater mitigation—are distributed across a community. It is a composite score (0 to 100) based on tree canopy cover, surface temperature, income, employment, race, age, and health factors. A lower score indicates a greater need for investment in urban forestry.

### Donna, TX
- **Composite Score:** 71 / 100
- **Tree Canopy Cover:** 14%
- **Neighborhood Disparities:** All of the city's block groups are currently below the optimal "100" Tree Equity Score threshold. Specifically, 10 out of 17 block groups have a score below 75, indicating a significant inequity in canopy distribution.
- **Improvement Goals:** To achieve a minimum score of 75 across all block groups, Donna requires an estimated 0.3 square miles of canopy expansion (approximately 15,739 trees). Reaching full equity (a score of 100) would require expanding the urban canopy by about 1.9 square miles (roughly 86,743 trees).

### Mercedes, TX
- **Tree Canopy Cover:** 15.4%
- **Current Status:** Similar to Donna, Mercedes exhibits lower tree equity in its socioeconomically disadvantaged block groups. 
- **Recent Initiatives:** The city has begun taking steps to address its canopy deficit, notably participating in a grant program through the Texas A&M Forest Service that provided 600 native trees for city parks, walking trails, and residential areas to improve green infrastructure.

## 3. Historical Municipal Infrastructure & Canopy Investment
The history of urban tree canopy in the Rio Grande Valley is characterized by massive ecological transformation. 

- **From Thornforest to Agriculture:** Historically, the region was covered by the Tamaulipan thornforest, a dense and biodiverse ecosystem. In the early 1900s, large-scale land clearing for irrigated agriculture decimated this landscape, leaving less than 5-10% of native habitats intact.
- **Rapid Urbanization:** Over the past few decades, intense urban growth has converted agricultural lands into cities. Municipal infrastructure investments historically prioritized drainage systems designed for agricultural fields and concrete-heavy urban development (e.g., expansive parking lots and wide roadways) over green infrastructure.
- **Lack of Canopy Investment:** Historically, there was a stark lack of municipal investment in maintaining or expanding the urban tree canopy. The shift from permeable soil to dark-colored impervious surfaces like asphalt and concrete occurred without parallel mandates for shade trees, leading to a severe "tree canopy deficit" across Hidalgo County.

## 4. Disproportionate Impact of the Urban Heat Island Effect
The Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect occurs when urbanized areas experience significantly higher temperatures than surrounding rural landscapes due to heat-absorbing infrastructure. In the RGV, one of the most economically disadvantaged regions in Texas, this creates a "compounded risk" for lower-income residents.

- **Infrastructure Inequities:** Lower-income neighborhoods in Donna and Mercedes frequently lack natural cooling features like mature tree canopies and parks. Instead, they feature a high concentration of heat-absorbing surfaces that trap heat during the day and release it slowly at night, preventing nighttime cooling.
- **Financial Barriers to Cooling:** The financial burden of mechanical cooling is high. Many low-income families face energy insecurity, forcing them to choose between high electrical bills and risking heat-related illnesses by keeping air conditioning off.
- **Vulnerability of Pedestrians and Transit Users:** Residents in these areas often rely on active transport or public transit and face a severe lack of shaded public infrastructure (like bus shelters or shaded sidewalks), increasing their direct exposure to dangerous heat.

### Ongoing Mitigation Efforts
To address these disparities, local initiatives are combining environmental data with socioeconomic insights:
- **Data-Driven Mapping:** Organizations like the Rio Grande International Study Center (RGISC) and researchers from UTRGV are conducting localized heat mapping to identify the hottest neighborhoods and prioritize interventions.
- **Nature-Based Solutions:** Programs like the "Shaded ReLeaf Project" aim to increase climate resilience by strategically planting native, drought-resistant thornforest species (such as Texas ebony and retama) in underserved, high-heat communities.

## Conclusion
The data clearly demonstrates that tree canopy is not just an aesthetic amenity but a critical piece of public health infrastructure. In Donna and Mercedes, targeted municipal investments in urban forestry are essential to improve Tree Equity Scores, mitigate the Urban Heat Island effect, and protect vulnerable lower-income communities from the escalating impacts of extreme heat.
