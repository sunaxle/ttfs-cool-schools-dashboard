Language, Rhetoric & Creative Expression in the Forest

While environmental education often focuses narrowly on STEM biology, a child's relationship with trees is deeply linguistic, poetic, historical, and visual. This portal maps all 7 Strands of Texas ELAR and 4 Strands of Texas Fine Arts, identifies critical educational gaps, and surveys pre-existing historical curricula.

Four Major Educational Gaps in Environmental Pedagogy

Our statewide curriculum audit revealed four primary areas where standard schoolyard activities fall short of meeting the full rigor of Texas ELAR and Fine Arts standards:

⚠️ What Curricula Misses ELAR Strands 6 & 7

Gap 1: The Civic Writing & Environmental Rhetoric Gap

Most environmental programs treat writing simply as "write a 4-line poem about a tree." Middle and High School students are rarely taught to use language as an authentic instrument of civic power, municipal advocacy, or investigative journalism.

Untouched TEKS: ELAR 6.12D, ELAR 8.12D, Eng I.11C, Eng III.11C (Argumentative & Civic Composition).
✓ Forest Literacy Solution

Module: "The Civic Pen: Municipal Shade Advocacy & Community Letters"

Students analyze neighborhood heat disparities, draft formal evidence-based letters to school boards, and publish op-eds for shade equity.

⚠️ What Curricula Misses Art Strand 3

Gap 2: The Regional Ethnobotanical & Indigenous Arts Gap

Standard art curriculum relies almost exclusively on temperate East Coast/European flora (maples, oaks, apples). South Texas students are not taught the profound cultural art traditions of their own semi-arid thornscrub ecosystem.

Untouched TEKS: Fine Arts 3.3A, Fine Arts 5.3B, Art II.3A (Historical & Cultural Context in Visual Art).
✓ Forest Literacy Solution

Module: "Roots of the RGV: Coahuiltecan Pigments & Eco-Canvas"

Students forage non-living mesquite pods and prickly pear juice to create non-toxic natural watercolors while exploring regional borderland folk art.

⚠️ What Curricula Misses ELAR Strands 1 & 3

Gap 3: The Bilingual & Sensory Journaling Gap

Existing nature journaling materials are heavily monolingual English, ignoring the rich linguistic heritage of the Rio Grande Valley (English, Español, and regional TexMex) where dual-language vocabulary thrives.

Untouched TEKS: ELAR K.1B, ELAR 2.1E, ELAR 4.7B (Oral Language & Personal Response in Nature).
✓ Forest Literacy Solution

Module: "Diario de la Naturaleza: Tri-Lingual Sit Spot Field Notebooks"

Provides rich dual-language sensory prompt banks (fresco, áspero, sombra, crujiente) encouraging authentic personal voice.

⚠️ What Curricula Misses Art Strands 1 & 2

Gap 4: The Data-Driven Visual Eco-Art Gap

Art in schools is often isolated from scientific data. Students draw trees purely from imagination rather than translating real-world environmental measurements (thermal scans, growth rings) into visual form.

Untouched TEKS: Fine Arts 6.2A, Fine Arts 8.2A, Art I.2C (Translating Observation into Visual Media).
✓ Forest Literacy Solution

Module: "Thermal Topography & Tree Ring Scientific Art Plates"

Students combine infrared temperature readings with tree-ring radial measurements to create scientific botanical plates.

The 7 Integrated Strands of Texas ELAR (19 TAC Chapter 110)

Texas law organizes reading, writing, speaking, and listening into seven interwoven strands across every grade level from Kindergarten through 12th grade:

Strand 1: Developing & Sustaining Foundational Language Skills

Oral language, listening comprehension, phonics, and contextual vocabulary acquisition in physical outdoor settings.

Forest Module: Tree Hugger Sensory Vocabulary Walk (K–2)

Strand 2: Comprehension Skills (Listening, Speaking, Reading & Writing)

Deep reading of informational texts, asking self-directed questions, predicting outcomes, and synthesizing multi-paragraph forestry texts.

Forest Module: Roots of the RGV: Ethnobotany Reading & Text-to-World (3–5)

Strand 3: Response Skills (Reflections & Evidence Citing)

Expressing emotional, artistic, and analytical responses to nature in field notebooks, paraphrasing observations, and supporting ideas with evidence.

Forest Module: Sensory Nature Journaling & Sit Spot Field Records (3–5)

Strand 4: Multiple Genres (Poetry, Memoirs, Informational, Folktales)

Analyzing traditional indigenous stories, nature memoirs (Aldo Leopold), informational science articles, and structured poetry.

Forest Module: Indigenous Pechita Oral Histories & American Ecocriticism (6–12)

Strand 5: Author's Purpose & Craft (Imagery, Tone, Rhetoric)

Analyzing how writers use metaphors, sensory imagery, personification, and rhetorical tone to depict ecosystems and shape public environmental attitudes.

Forest Module: From 'Weed' to Keystone: Mesquite Rhetorical Analysis (6–8)

Strand 6: Composition (Writing Process, Poetry & Argumentation)

Drafting, revising, and editing personal memoirs, nature Haiku, and formal persuasive essays addressing public shade equity and tree preservation.

Forest Module: The Civic Pen: Municipal Shade Advocacy & Op-Eds (9–12)

Strand 7: Inquiry & Research (Student-Driven Field Investigations)

Formulating authentic scientific questions about campus microclimates, gathering primary field data, evaluating sources, and presenting conclusions.

Forest Module: Campus Shade Equity & Temperature Investigative Report (6–12)

The 4 Core Strands of Texas Visual Arts (19 TAC Chapter 117)

Visual Arts standards build perceptual awareness, technical media skills, cultural appreciation, and reflective critique:

Strand 1: Foundations: Observation & Perception

Developing sensitivity to natural textures, branching angles, leaf venation, color variations in native bark, and dappled light values (*Komorebi*).

Forest Module: Dappled Sunlight & Cast Shadow Value Studies (3–5)

Strand 2: Creative Expression (Media, Technique & Form)

Creating artwork using graphite, watercolors, wax bark rubbings, natural plant dyes, and sustainable site-specific land art installations.

Forest Module: Precision Botanical Scientific Illustration Plates (6–8)

Strand 3: Historical & Cultural Relevance

Exploring how indigenous, Mexican-American, and regional South Texas cultures have represented trees and natural landscapes across centuries of history.

Forest Module: Sacred Ahuehuete Iconography & Borderland Mural Traditions (6–12)

Strand 4: Critical Evaluation & Response

Analyzing, critiquing, and curating schoolyard eco-art galleries; reflecting on how public landscape design influences emotional peace.

Forest Module: Campus Microforest Eco-Gallery & Aesthetics Critique (9–12)

Pre-Existing Educational Material Lineage & Attributions

Giving formal academic and pedagogical credit to foundational programs in environmental literature and botanical arts:

Project Learning Tree (PLT)

Pioneered foundational activities: Poet-Tree (Activity 5), Adopt a Tree (Activity 21), and The Closer You Look (Activity 61). Established early models of outdoor journaling.

Citation: Project Learning Tree (2020) Forest Literacy Framework

John Muir Laws Nature Journaling

World-renowned visual journaling methodology (*"I Notice, I Wonder, It Reminds Me Of"*). Bridges scientific drawing with observational inquiry.

Citation: Laws & Lygren (2020) How to Teach Nature Journaling

Aldo Leopold Foundation

*The Land Ethic* curriculum exploring environmental philosophy, ecological conscience, and the literary tradition of nature essays.

Citation: Leopold, A. (1949) A Sand County Almanac

Robin Wall Kimmerer

Pioneered Indigenous Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) in modern literature, examining linguistic kinship and reciprocal stewardship with flora.

Citation: Kimmerer, R. W. (2013) Braiding Sweetgrass
